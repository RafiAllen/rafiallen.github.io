Crazyflie Flight Control: From Simulation to Hardware

Summer Undergraduate Research Program (SURP) · Cal Poly San Luis Obispo

Goal. Take a controller tuned in simulation and test whether a physical Crazyflie could climb 1 meter, fly forward 1 meter, and land.

Work. I developed and tuned a cascaded PID flight controller, building on an existing extended Kalman filter (EKF) and flight-tracking framework from prior research. In Isaac Lab, I used a digital twin of Bitcraze’s Crazyflie 2.1+ micro aerial vehicle (MAV) to test the full flight sequence: climb to 1 m, hover, fly forward 1 m, and land.

After the controller completed the sequence in simulation, I transferred the control logic to the physical Crazyflie using a Python workflow with PyTorch and cflib. I configured Lighthouse positioning with a SteamVR 2.0 base station to provide measurements for onboard state estimation. I then analyzed flight telemetry to investigate lateral drift and refine the controller gains for different phases of flight.

Tools

  • Simulation: Isaac Lab and a Crazyflie digital twin
  • Control software: Python and PyTorch
  • Flight commands and telemetry: cflib and Crazyradio
  • Flight hardware: Bitcraze Crazyflie 2.1+ MAV
  • Localization: Lighthouse positioning with a SteamVR 2.0 base station

Results

I evaluated the cascaded PID controller across five Crazyflie hardware flights. The mission was to climb to 1 m, hover, fly forward 1 m, and land. The Crazyflie finished an average of 0.955 m forward of its starting point, 4.5 cm short of the target. The average of the five individual peak altitudes was 1.077 m, or 7.7 cm above the commanded height.

Crazyflie Reinforcement Learning in Isaac Lab

Goal. Evaluate how well reinforcement learning policies could hold a fixed hover and follow a moving reference trajectory on a simulated Crazyflie.

Work. I trained and evaluated a PPO policy for fixed-position hover. In a separate trajectory-tracking experiment, I compared saved PPO, SAC, and TD3 policies across 64 simulated environments per policy. I used evaluator summaries and recorded rollouts to examine position error, command saturation, and low-altitude failure flags.

Tools

  • Simulation. NVIDIA Isaac Lab and a Crazyflie digital twin
  • Training. Python, PyTorch, RSL-RL for hover, and skrl for trajectory tracking
  • Analysis. TensorBoard, MCAP rollout logs, and evaluator summaries

Results

Fixed-position hover. The PPO policy achieved a 3D position root mean square error (RMSE) of 13.6 mm in a five-second, fixed-start simulation test. The mean distance from the target at the end of the test was 14.7 mm, and all 64 evaluation environments completed the test without a crash flag.

Trajectory tracking. In a separate experiment, I evaluated PPO, SAC, and TD3 on a moving reference trajectory. Each saved policy was evaluated across 64 simulated environments using a 10-second evaluation window.

What the comparison showed. PPO had the lowest tracking error among the tested policies. PPO and SAC both avoided the configured low-altitude failure condition, but SAC operated at its command limits much more often. Command saturation measures how often at least one normalized command reached its limit. TD3 triggered the failure condition in every evaluation environment.
